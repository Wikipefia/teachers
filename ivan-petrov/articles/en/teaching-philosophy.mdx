# My Teaching Philosophy

After 15 years of teaching mathematics at the university level, I still find the moment of understanding in a student's eyes to be the most rewarding part of my career.

<Callout type="info">
This article is for students curious about **why** I teach the way I do. It may also be useful for fellow educators.
</Callout>

## Why I Teach

Mathematics is often feared, but it doesn't have to be. My goal is to make abstract concepts tangible and intuitive. When a student says "I finally get it," that's the moment that keeps me going.

## The Visual Approach

I believe in teaching through geometric intuition first, formalism second.

<Tabs>
<Tab label="Traditional Approach">
Start with definition → prove theorem → show example → assign exercises.

This works for students who already have mathematical maturity, but it often alienates newcomers.
</Tab>

<Tab label="My Approach">
Start with a picture → ask "what do you see?" → build intuition → formalize → prove.

By the time we reach the proof, students already *expect* the result. The proof becomes confirmation, not revelation.
</Tab>
</Tabs>

> **For My Students**: Every linear algebra concept has a geometric meaning. If I haven't shown you the picture yet, ask me — I guarantee it exists.

## On Making Mistakes

Mistakes are not failures — they're data. Every wrong answer tells you something about your understanding.

<Callout type="warning">
I never penalize students for wrong answers during lectures or office hours. The only "bad" question is the one you didn't ask.
</Callout>

## Five Rules for My Students

<Collapse title="1. Work problems by hand before using software">
NumPy is a tool, not a teacher. When you compute a matrix inverse by hand, you understand what "singular" means viscerally. When NumPy throws `LinAlgError`, you'll know exactly why.
</Collapse>

<Collapse title="2. Form study groups">
Teaching others is the best way to learn. If you can explain eigenvalues to a friend at a café, you truly understand them. If you can't, you know exactly what to study next.
</Collapse>

<Collapse title="3. Come to office hours with specific questions">
"I don't understand Chapter 3" is hard to help with. "I don't understand why the determinant changes sign when you swap rows" — that I can work with in 5 minutes.
</Collapse>

<Collapse title="4. Read the textbook before the lecture, not after">
Lectures work best when you've already seen the material once. You don't need to understand everything on first read — just get familiar with the vocabulary and the shape of the ideas.
</Collapse>

<Collapse title="5. Don't memorize formulas — understand derivations">
If you understand *where* the quadratic formula comes from (completing the square), you'll never forget it. If you just memorize it, you'll blank during the exam.
</Collapse>
